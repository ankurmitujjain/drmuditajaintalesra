## Can I have a normal delivery?

**Most women with a healthy, low-risk pregnancy can have a safe normal (vaginal) delivery.** A normal birth usually means a quicker recovery, a shorter hospital stay and earlier bonding and breastfeeding. Caesarean section is an important, life-saving operation when it is medically needed, and the aim is to perform it **for the right reasons**.

Dr. Mudita Jain Talesra supports women to plan for a normal birth wherever it is safe, with clear guidance at every step.

## How can you prepare for a normal delivery?

| Pregnancy stage | What helps |
|---|---|
| Before pregnancy | Pre-pregnancy check, folic acid, control of thyroid, diabetes and BP |
| First trimester | Booking visit, dating scan, screening tests |
| Second trimester | Anomaly scan, glucose test, safe exercise, healthy weight gain |
| Third trimester | Growth monitoring, birth-preparation counselling, breathing and positioning practice |
| Labour | Supportive care, mobility, pain-relief options, continuous monitoring when needed |

Practical steps that help:
- **Regular antenatal check-ups** (see [Pregnancy Care](/obstetrics-services/))
- **Staying active**: walking and pregnancy-safe exercise, unless advised otherwise
- **Healthy weight gain** and good control of blood sugar and blood pressure
- **Birth preparation**: knowing the signs of labour, breathing techniques and positions
- **Allowing labour to start naturally** unless there is a medical reason to induce

## What is painless delivery (epidural)?

**Painless delivery usually refers to epidural analgesia.** An anaesthetist places a very thin tube in the lower back, near the nerves that carry labour pain. Medicine given through this tube **reduces pain significantly**, while you stay awake, aware and able to push.

### Benefits
- Effective, adjustable pain relief through labour
- Lets you rest and conserve energy
- Can be used for an assisted delivery or converted for a caesarean if needed

### Things to know
- Not every woman needs or wants an epidural, and other pain-relief options exist
- Possible side effects include a fall in blood pressure, temporary leg heaviness, itching, fever or (rarely) headache
- It isn't suitable for everyone, for example with some bleeding disorders or spine problems
- It requires a hospital with an anaesthetist available

Epidural availability depends on the hospital where you deliver. This is discussed during antenatal visits.

## What other pain-relief options are there?

- Breathing techniques, movement, upright positions and massage
- Warm compresses and a supportive birth companion
- Other medical options, as available at the hospital

## What are the signs of labour?

Go to the hospital or contact the doctor if you have:
- **Regular, painful contractions** getting stronger and closer together
- **Leaking of water** (rupture of membranes)
- **Bleeding**
- **Reduced baby movements**
- Severe headache, blurred vision or swelling (possible high blood pressure)

## Normal delivery after caesarean (VBAC)

Many women who had one previous caesarean can safely try for a **vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC)**. Suitability depends on the reason for the earlier caesarean, the type of scar, the interval since then and the current pregnancy. VBAC must take place in a hospital ready for emergency caesarean. A personalised discussion helps you decide.

## After delivery

Postnatal care covers recovery, stitch care, breastfeeding support, contraception advice and emotional wellbeing. Watch for warning signs such as heavy bleeding, fever, severe pain or low mood.
