## What is PCOS?

**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal condition in women of reproductive age.** The ovaries produce more male-type hormones (androgens) than usual, and insulin often works less effectively in the body (insulin resistance). As a result, eggs may not be released regularly, which leads to irregular periods and difficulty conceiving.

Despite the name, you don't need cysts on your ovaries to have PCOS, and having small follicles on an ultrasound doesn't by itself mean you have PCOS.

## What are the symptoms of PCOS?

- **Irregular, delayed or missed periods** (cycles longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year)
- **Acne** and oily skin
- **Excess facial or body hair** (hirsutism)
- **Thinning scalp hair**
- **Weight gain**, especially around the waist, and difficulty losing weight
- **Dark, velvety skin patches** on the neck or underarms (acanthosis nigricans)
- **Difficulty conceiving**
- Mood changes, anxiety or low self-esteem

## How is PCOS diagnosed?

Doctors commonly use the **Rotterdam criteria**: PCOS is diagnosed when at least **two of these three** are present, after other causes have been ruled out:

1. Irregular or absent ovulation (irregular periods)
2. Signs of high androgens (acne, excess hair) or raised androgen levels in blood tests
3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound

**Tests usually include** a pelvic ultrasound, thyroid function (TSH), prolactin, androgen levels where needed, and blood sugar and lipid tests to check metabolic health. For adolescent girls, diagnosis is made more carefully, because irregular cycles are common in the first years after periods begin.

## When should you see a gynaecologist?

See a doctor if your periods stay irregular more than 2–3 years after they began, you go more than 3 months without a period, you notice increasing facial hair or acne, you are gaining weight rapidly, or you have been trying to conceive for 6–12 months without success.

## How is PCOS treated?

Treatment is tailored to your main concern: regular periods, skin and hair, weight, or pregnancy.

### 1. Lifestyle changes (the foundation for everyone)
- **Balanced diet:** more vegetables, pulses, whole grains and protein; fewer refined carbohydrates, sugar and fried foods. Traditional Indian meals can be adapted easily.
- **Exercise:** at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, plus muscle-strengthening exercise.
- **Sleep and stress management.**
- **Weight management:** if overweight, a 5–10% weight loss can make a real difference.

### 2. Regulating periods
- **Hormonal tablets** (combined oral contraceptives) to regularise cycles and reduce acne and hair growth, for women not trying to conceive.
- **Progesterone courses** to bring on a period and protect the uterine lining.
- **Insulin-sensitising medicine** (such as metformin) for selected women with insulin resistance.

### 3. Treating acne and excess hair
Hormonal treatment, anti-androgen medicines where appropriate, and cosmetic hair-removal options. Results take several months to show.

### 4. PCOS and pregnancy
For women who want to conceive:
- **Ovulation induction** with tablets such as letrozole, with **follicular monitoring** (serial ultrasound) to time intercourse.
- **IUI** (intrauterine insemination) in selected couples.
- **Laparoscopic ovarian drilling** in specific cases that don't respond to medicines.
- Referral for IVF when needed.

## PCOS in teenagers

Irregular periods are common in the first two years after periods start. Teenagers with persistent irregular cycles, severe acne or excess hair growth should be evaluated gently and confidentially. Early lifestyle guidance can prevent future problems. Mothers are welcome to accompany their daughters.
