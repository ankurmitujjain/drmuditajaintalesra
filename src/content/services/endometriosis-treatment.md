## What is endometriosis?

**Endometriosis is a chronic, oestrogen-dependent condition in which tissue similar to the uterine lining (endometrium) grows outside the uterus.** It is commonly found on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, the ligaments behind the uterus and the pelvic lining, and sometimes on the bowel or bladder. This tissue responds to monthly hormones and bleeds, which causes inflammation, pain and adhesions (internal scar tissue).

Endometriosis affects roughly **1 in 10 women of reproductive age**, yet the diagnosis is often delayed by years because period pain is wrongly considered "normal".

## What are the symptoms?

- **Severe period pain (dysmenorrhoea)** that worsens over time
- **Chronic pelvic or lower back pain**
- **Pain during or after intercourse** (dyspareunia)
- **Painful bowel movements or urination**, especially during periods
- **Heavy or irregular bleeding**
- **Difficulty conceiving**
- Fatigue and bloating

The severity of pain doesn't always match the extent of the disease. Some women with mild disease have severe pain, and some with advanced disease have few symptoms.

## When should you see a gynaecologist?

Book a consultation if period pain makes you miss school, college or work, painkillers no longer help, intercourse is painful, or you have been trying to conceive without success. **Sudden severe pelvic pain with fever, vomiting or fainting needs urgent care.**

## How is endometriosis diagnosed?

1. **Detailed history** of pain, periods and fertility.
2. **Pelvic examination.**
3. **Transvaginal ultrasound** to detect endometriomas (chocolate cysts) and signs of deep disease.
4. **MRI pelvis** in selected cases, to plan surgery.
5. **Diagnostic laparoscopy**, which can confirm and treat endometriosis in the same procedure.

Modern guidelines allow treatment to begin based on symptoms and imaging, without waiting for surgery to confirm the diagnosis.

## How is endometriosis treated?

The plan depends on your main goal: **pain relief, fertility, or both.**

### 1. Pain relief
Anti-inflammatory painkillers taken around periods, with heat therapy, exercise and physiotherapy where helpful.

### 2. Hormonal treatment
These medicines reduce or stop periods, which calms the disease:
- **Progestogens** (for example dienogest)
- **Combined hormonal pills**, taken continuously or cyclically
- **Levonorgestrel intrauterine system** (hormonal IUD)
- **GnRH analogues**, for short-term use in selected cases

Hormonal treatment controls symptoms well for many women but is not used while trying to conceive.

### 3. Laparoscopic (keyhole) surgery
Laparoscopy is advised when pain persists despite medicines, cysts are large, the diagnosis is uncertain, or as part of fertility treatment. During surgery:
- Endometriosis deposits are **excised or ablated**
- **Chocolate cysts are removed**, carefully preserving healthy ovarian tissue
- **Adhesions are released** to restore normal anatomy
- Tubal patency can be tested at the same time

### 4. Endometriosis and fertility
Depending on age, ovarian reserve (AMH), tubal status and partner factors, options include expectant management after surgery, ovulation induction with IUI, or referral for IVF. See [Infertility Evaluation & Treatment](/fertility-services/).

## Treatment options at a glance

| Situation | Usual approach |
|---|---|
| Painful periods, no cyst | Painkillers and hormonal treatment |
| Pain not controlled by medicines | Laparoscopic excision, then hormonal maintenance |
| Large or growing chocolate cyst | Laparoscopic cystectomy, preserving the ovary |
| Trying to conceive | Fertility evaluation; surgery, IUI or IVF as appropriate |

## Living with endometriosis

Endometriosis is a long-term condition, but it can be managed. Continuing hormonal treatment after surgery (when not trying to conceive), staying active, eating a balanced diet and keeping up regular follow-up all help reduce recurrence and keep you in control.
